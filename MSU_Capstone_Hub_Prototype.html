Why sponsor

What a sponsor gets out of it

Beyond a finished deliverable, a capstone puts your work in front of more students than the team that builds it — these projects are presented at Design Day, used in classes, and known across the college. It's a low-cost way to become familiar to the engineers your industry will hire from.

01

Workforce development

Students graduate already familiar with the problems, tools, and standards your industry uses. Sponsoring is a small way to strengthen the talent pool you and your peers hire from.

02

Fresh approaches, low-risk

A strong senior team with faculty backing gives you room to explore — pursuing a promising idea, prototyping something speculative, or testing an assumption — with real engineering effort at modest cost. It's a low-risk way to investigate questions worth a closer look.

03

Standing with future decision-makers

Many of today's seniors go on to specify tools, choose vendors, and sign contracts, and the faculty who advise them come to know your company. Sponsoring is an early, low-key way to build those relationships.

04

Familiarity across a cohort

Capstone projects are presented at Design Day, referenced in classes, and often known by students before their senior year. That means your work reaches more students than the team on the project — many of whom you'll later see as applicants already familiar with the kind of problems you work on.

For students

What students get out of it

A sponsored capstone is the closest thing to professional practice that a degree offers. Students take responsibility for a real problem, a real client, and a result that has to work.

01

A real, open-ended problem

An open-ended, ambiguous problem from a real organization — scoping it, making engineering trade-offs, and owning the outcome over a full semester.

02

Experience with a real client

Working directly with a sponsor means managing expectations, presenting progress, and adapting to constraints and feedback — the professional skills coursework rarely teaches, learned on an actual engagement.

03

Something to show for it

A finished deliverable, a Design Day presentation, and a concrete project they can point to with employers — plus a clearer sense of how a particular industry actually works, and whether it fits them.

Sponsored projects

Projects take many forms

There is no single template for a sponsored project. The examples below illustrate the range; a department coordinator will work with you to shape your idea, whatever form it takes, into a one-semester scope with defined deliverables.

A current engineering problem

A design, build, analysis, or software challenge your organization is actively working on or has deferred for lack of capacity.

Example: redesign a test fixture; build an internal tool; evaluate three design alternatives

A problem you've already solved

A challenge your team previously addressed, approached independently by students as a point of comparison. Useful where confidentiality limits what can be shared about current work.

Example: a component design or process problem from a recent program

An area you're evaluating

A technology survey, feasibility study, or benchmarking analysis in a space your organization is considering but has not staffed.

Example: assess AI-based visual inspection methods; benchmark competitor approaches

A project for the community

Your organization funds a project for a Michigan nonprofit or public agency that could not otherwise commission engineering work. MSU identifies and matches the partner organization.

Example: assistive equipment for an adaptive sports program; a tool for a regional food bank

If your idea does not resemble any of these, submit it as written. Projects are shaped collaboratively during scoping, and sample project briefs from past semesters are available on request.

How it works

The sponsorship process

01

Submit an inquiry

Provide your organization, a contact, and a brief description of your interest.

02

Scope the project

A department coordinator works with you to define a one-semester scope and deliverables.

03

Work with the team

A team of 4–6 seniors works on the project through the semester, with regular sponsor check-ins.

04

Design Day

The team presents its results at Design Day and delivers final documentation.

Typically one semester 4–6 students per team $5,000 project budget ~1 hr/week sponsor time Interdisciplinary teams available
This describes the single-semester, company-sponsored model. A few tracks differ — BAE runs over two semesters, and Civil and Chemical Engineering use predefined department projects. See Departments for how each one works.
