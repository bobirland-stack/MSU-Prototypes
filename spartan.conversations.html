What a good conversation looks like

For students

  • You have a clearer picture of a career path and what it would take to get there
  • You understand what a role involves day to day
  • You have a specific next step
  • You've added a professional contact
  • You've practiced a professional conversation

For alumni

  • You gave a student a clearer picture than they could get elsewhere
  • You met a student in your field
  • You stayed connected to the College
  • Your time commitment stayed within what you agreed to

Time commitment

Alumni set their own capacity, usually two or three conversations a semester. Students can have up to two open requests at a time, and most have two to four conversations a year with people in different fields. Nobody commits to anything beyond the scheduled conversation. There is no cost to participate.

If a conversation goes well

Alumni sometimes offer a second conversation, a resume review, or an introduction. That is how longer mentoring relationships tend to begin, and it happens at the alum's initiative.

Verification and privacy

Students verify enrollment with their @msu.edu email. Alumni sign up themselves, and College of Engineering staff review each profile before it appears. Contact information isn't shown in the directory. When an alum accepts a request and a time is settled, both people receive each other's email; the student sends the calendar invitation with a video meeting link, and they coordinate directly from there. Both parties agree to the , and concerns can be reported to College of Engineering staff. Video calls on Teams and Zoom support live captions; for other accommodations, contact engr.conversations@msu.edu.

The short version

An informational interview is a conversation with someone doing work you're considering. You're there to learn from their experience, not to ask for a job. Everyone in the directory has agreed to one 25-minute conversation; they have not agreed to make introductions or review resumes, though some will offer. Accept if they do, and don't ask.

1 Prepare ten minutes, the day before
2 Introduce yourself one minute

Four parts, one or two sentences each: your year and major; one or two relevant things you've done; the decision you're working through; why you chose them.

Example

"I'm a junior in mechanical engineering. I've spent two semesters on the Formula SAE team, mostly on powertrain, and I had a summer internship at a supplier that I liked more than I expected. I'm trying to decide whether to aim for design roles or manufacturing roles after graduation, and I wanted to talk with you because you've worked in both."

That gives them a clear starting point. From there, go to your first question.

3 What to ask their experience and judgment

Ask how they made decisions, what they learned, and what they'd suggest for someone in your position. Avoid anything the company website answers, and anything so broad the honest answer is "it depends."

  • What does your work involve day to day, and what takes up most of your time?
  • How did you decide on this path over the alternatives you were considering?
  • What did you learn about this kind of work only after you started doing it?
  • What do people who do well in your field tend to do differently?
  • If you were in my position, what would you focus on over the next year?

Adjust them to the person. The request form suggests questions by career stage too.

4 During 25 minutes
  • Take the lead: open with your introduction and your first question. Listen more than you talk, and follow up on what they say.
  • Take notes. Keep to the time; at about twenty minutes, move to your last question and thank them.
5 Follow up within a day

Send a thank-you that mentions one specific thing you took from the conversation and what you plan to do with it, then a LinkedIn connection request that references the conversation.

Template

Hi [name],

Thank you for taking the time to talk yesterday. [One specific thing you took from the conversation.] Based on what you said, I'm planning to [the next step you're taking].

I've sent you a connection request on LinkedIn. I'd like to keep you posted on how this goes.

Best,
[your name]

Stay in touch occasionally without asking for anything: when you act on their advice, land an internship, or graduate.

Sending the invitation

After the alum accepts and you pick one of the times they offered, you send the calendar invitation. The connection email includes a ready-to-paste version. In Outlook or Google Calendar, create an event at the agreed time, add the alum's email as a guest, paste the invitation text, and add a video link: in Outlook, turn on the Teams meeting toggle; in Google Calendar, add a Google Meet link; or paste a Zoom link from your MSU Zoom account. Send it the same day. If you've never done this, the Center can walk you through it in five minutes.

6 Common mistakes
  • Treating it as a job interview, or asking for a referral or an introduction.
  • Arriving without questions, or asking things their profile already answers.
  • Going over time.
  • Skipping the follow-up.

Making the conversation useful

1 Read the request first two minutes

Start from the student's questions rather than a general introduction. They wrote why they chose you; lead with that part of your story.

2 Be specific including what didn't go to plan

Students learn the most from how you made decisions, what surprised you, and what you would do differently. Include the parts that didn't go to plan.

3 Ask what they're uncertain about not only what they're interested in

That's usually where you can help most.

4 If they ask about jobs you can't help with

Say so plainly and tell them what you would do in their position.

5 Close with one suggestion if you have one

Something to read, someone to look into, or a next step.

If you host a session: a one-page guide

Prepare four things. The brief you claimed tells you the topic; these are the parts every session needs.

  • One specific day: a real Tuesday, a real problem, the tools you used.
  • One mistake or struggle from your first two years, and how it went. This is required, not optional; students learn more from it than from anything that went well.
  • If it's true, one moment you felt you didn't belong, and how that faded.
  • One next step you'll invite them to take, with a real time and place.

The 45 minutes. Questions first, not a talk.

  • 0 to 5: names, and one thing each student is curious about. Before you say anything.
  • 5 to 15: your day, and what the job actually involves.
  • 15 to 20: the mistake, and that it's normal.
  • 20 to 40: their questions; you'll have the ones they sent the day before.
  • 40 to 45: the next step, and have each student say when and where they'll do it: "Thursday at 4, the Formula SAE open meeting, room 1220." Saying it out loud is what makes it happen.

Logistics. You supply the video link or, for a cohort, they supply the room. With co-hosts, whoever created the session is the organizer: supplies the link and receives the questions, with co-hosts copied. Four to six students is the right size; eight is the most; three with questions is a good session.

Don't: narrate a highlight reel; imply you were always confident; promise in the room more than the title did.

Community standards

Spartan Engineering Conversations connects adult students with alumni for professional career conversations. These standards keep it that way.

Both parties agree to

  • Keep conversations professional and career-focused
  • Show up on time, or reschedule with notice by email. Repeated no-shows may pause a participant's access
  • Respect that either person may end a conversation or decline further contact at any time, without explanation
  • No soliciting: no sales, recruiting fees, investment pitches, or requests for money in either direction. Talking about how a company hires, and suggesting a student apply, is fine
  • Treat personal information shared in conversation as private
  • Meet in-person conversations in public settings, such as a campus building, a coffee shop, or a workplace
  • Cancel a session seat in advance if you can't attend; a held seat is a seat another student can't have

Verification

Students are verified through their MSU email. Alumni sign up themselves, and College of Engineering staff review each profile before it appears in the directory. Contact details are not published in the directory. They are shared between the two people when a request is accepted.

Reporting a concern

Any participant can report a concern to College of Engineering staff at any time. Reports are reviewed by College of Engineering staff, and participants who violate these standards lose access to the directory. Reports involving harassment or discrimination are referred through MSU's established Office for Civil Rights processes.

Each session's row shows the four numbers that matter: signed up, showed up, next steps completed (an advising appointment booked or a team meeting attended, from the Center's records or a one-tap reply), and returned for a later session. The seat check-in carries one extra item for first-years, "I can picture what an engineer actually does," on a five-point scale. Cohort leaders' check-ins carry one line the Center reads as color, not as a measure: what the room wanted to do next.
