Test your parts against real radiation, and build the space-electronics workforce.

The MSU Space Electronics Initiative is an industry-membership research center for the design and testing of electronic devices, circuits, and systems that operate in space. Members test at FRIB, collaborate with MSU faculty and students, and hire from a growing talent pipeline. The College of Engineering leads it jointly with the Facility for Rare Isotope Beams.

Three ways to work with us

What we do

Design and test electronics for the harsh conditions of space

Faculty, students, and researchers work with industry and government on devices, circuits, and systems that have to survive radiation and extreme environments, from Low Earth Orbit and beyond. The Initiative rests on three things.

Pillar one

MSU engineering and science talent

Interdisciplinary faculty, students, and researchers across the College of Engineering and the sciences, most in Electrical and Computer Engineering.

Pillar two

FRIB testing at MSU

The Facility for Rare Isotope Beams gives members a place to test electronics against the kind of radiation they will face in space. FRIB is a separate MSU facility that the Initiative works with.

Pillar three

Industrial member knowledge

Member companies bring the problems, the parts, and the market pull. Their questions set much of what the Initiative works on.

Support

The Initiative is supported by the U.S. Department of Energy, whose Office of Science owns FRIB, operated by MSU.

The marquee asset

Radiation testing at FRIB

Qualifying electronics for space usually means finding a beam, waiting for time on it, and traveling to it. Members get monthly access to testing at FRIB, one of a small number of facilities in the world that can do this. Faster qualification means members can move parts toward flight sooner.

The speed argument

Membership shortens the path to flight

High-reliability, radiation-hardened electronics are in demand, and getting a part qualified is often the slow step. Regular access to FRIB testing shortens qualification, and the talent pipeline feeds engineers into member companies. The point is speed and capability, not slogans.

  • Test soonerMonthly FRIB access means you are not waiting months for beam time elsewhere.
  • Own the resultsA royalty-free license on shared results, or a separate IP arrangement for proprietary work.
  • Hire the peopleCapstones and exchanges give you a first look at engineers trained on exactly this work.

What members test

Electronics that have to survive space

Space subjects electronics to radiation that causes single-event effects and long-term damage. Testing at FRIB helps members see how parts respond before they fly.

Devices

Solid-state devices

The building blocks of satellite and spacecraft electronics, tested for radiation response.

Circuits

Integrated circuits

Analog and embedded parts, including the high-reliability packaging used in space.

Systems

Systems in context

How devices and circuits behave together under the conditions they will face in orbit and beyond.

A note on ownership

FRIB is a separate MSU facility with its own mandate and leadership. The Space Electronics Initiative works with FRIB to arrange member testing. We credit FRIB accurately and do not present its facility as our own.

Research and testing

Devices, circuits, and systems for the harsh conditions of space.

The Initiative works on the design and testing of electronics that operate in space, drawing on faculty across Electrical and Computer Engineering and the sciences, plus FRIB physicists. Below is the shape of the work and the people who lead it.

What we work on

Areas of work

The Initiative brings together several lines of engineering and science. The groupings below are a plain-language map of the work. Confirm groupings with the Initiative

Radiation effects

How electronics respond to radiation

Single-event effects and radiation testing of semiconductor devices, the core of qualifying parts for space. Faculty including Bill Harokopus work on heavy-ion testing of semiconductor devices.

RF and antennas

Radio-frequency and antenna systems

Conformal antennas, arrays, radomes, and phased arrays, the systems that let spacecraft communicate and sense.

Devices and materials

Device physics and materials

The physics of the devices themselves, including the materials that make them work and fail in extreme environments.

Electromagnetics

Applied electromagnetics

Computational electromagnetics and the field behavior that underpins antennas, circuits, and sensing.

How the work reaches the field

Shared results come with a perpetual non-exclusive royalty-free license. Members can also sponsor proprietary research with IP negotiated separately, and there is a path to commercialization through member agreements or new start-ups.

Students

Hands-on work in a high-demand field, sized to your commitment.

Space electronics needs people, and member companies hire from here. The Initiative gives students real work: senior capstones, faculty and student exchanges, radiation-testing boot camps, and workforce development. Here are the ways in.

Ways to get involved

Paths for students

These paths run through member collaboration, so many connect students directly to companies working on space electronics.

A semester

Senior capstone projects

Work a real problem with a member company as your capstone, on the same devices, circuits, and systems the Initiative tests.

A few days

Radiation-testing boot camp

The Single Event Effects boot camp, sponsored by Texas Instruments with FRIB, put students alongside industry and MSU engineers on real testing. Confirm next dates

A term or more

Faculty and student exchanges

Exchanges between MSU and member organizations, a way to spend time inside a company or bring their engineers here.

Ongoing

Workforce development

Training aimed at the in-demand space-electronics and affiliated science and engineering workforce, built with the companies that hire.
