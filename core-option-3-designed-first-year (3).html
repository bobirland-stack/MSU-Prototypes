First-Year Engineering CoRe Experience

The launchpad for Spartan Engineers.

CoRe brings together the cornerstone design courses, tutoring and advising, and the option to live among other engineers in South Neighborhood. It also puts first-year students in front of working engineers in year one. The aim is to build the habits and standards an engineering career depends on, starting in the first semester.

How CoRe works.

You are already in CoRe

Every incoming engineering student is part of the CoRe Experience. There is no application and nothing to sign up for. If you are taking EGR 100, this is for you.

Everyone thinks everyone else has it figured out

This is the most reliable thing anybody can tell you about a first year, and it comes from data, not from us trying to make you feel better. People consistently underestimate how much others liked talking to them. People consistently underestimate how glad someone would be to hear from them, and the effect is strongest when you barely know the person. And most people believe everyone else has a fuller social life than they do.

What that produces on a floor in October is everyone sitting in their room assuming everyone else is out, which is a lonely way to spend a term for no reason. The person you are nervous about messaging is probably having the same week you are.

Engineering housing is optional

Engineering students can choose to live together in Wilson, Wonders or Holden. Plenty of first-year engineers live elsewhere on campus or at home, and everything here applies either way.

Admission to major comes later

You are admitted to the college first and enter a specific degree program later. That step is called admission to major, it has its own credit and grade requirements that differ by major, and it is competitive. What we can and cannot claim about this role. The Fellow sits two steps from the student: a partner engineer coaches the Fellow, the Fellow coaches the team. Research on coaching says that first step works and that the second is not demonstrated, and in the best randomised evidence student-coaches showed no significant effect at all. So what travels between the layers is not a method the Fellow has to remember but a single page the team keeps current, on the same logic as a surgical checklist: the page does the remembering. We expect to see this role change the Fellows. Whether it changes the students they coach is a question we intend to measure honestly, and not yet a claim.

Context

How this relates to the teaching assistant role.

EGR 100 already has undergraduate teaching assistants supporting design teams, and that work continues as it is. For a smaller group, the Fellowship adds three things.

Industry funding

Partner companies fund the fellowship, which pays an additional stipend beyond the standard teaching assistant position and covers the training week before classes.

Industry training

The training is built with partner engineers around how design work runs in their organizations: iterating quickly, testing early, and reviewing a decision before it becomes expensive to change.

Assigned teams

Fellows stay with the same design teams all semester instead of floating across the course, so they see a project through from the first sketch to the final review.

Who owns what. Course faculty own the curriculum and the assessment. Teaching assistants continue in their role as they are now. Fellows coach the design teams they are assigned to.

The work

What a Design Coach Fellow does.

EGR 100 is where students take on their first open-ended engineering project with people they did not choose. Most of what makes that difficult is not technical, and that is the part you help with.

How teams work

Most first-year team trouble starts with unspoken expectations, not with anyone being difficult. You help teams name the problem early and agree who is doing what.

Working through an open problem

First-year projects have no answer key. You help teams break a problem down, decide what to try first, and keep going when the first version does not work.

Design reviews

At each milestone you run a practice defense: no screens, explain your decisions, take the questions. Grading stays with the instructor.

What a week looks like

  • In studio. Two sessions in the class period where teams work on their projects, alongside your assigned teams rather than presenting to them.
  • With faculty. One meeting with the course instructor and your fellow coaches to compare notes and plan the week.
  • Preparation. Reading the week's focus and reviewing where each of your teams got to.
  • Occasionally. A build night, a skill clinic with a partner engineer, or a session with a visiting alum.

Beyond the pay

What the role includes.

Alongside the paid coaching hours, fellows get training, an industry mentor, a credential and a reference.

Coaching training

A paid intensive of about 10 hours before the semester starts, covering facilitation, asking instead of answering, team conflict, recognizing when a student is struggling, and where to refer. Then one hour a week through the semester.Confirm

An industry mentor

An engineer from your pod's partner company, matched to your field, for a conversation each month during the fall or spring window.

Published research

The program publishes what it learns through national engineering-education venues, with fellows as co-authors.

A credential and a reference

A dean-signed credential on completion, and a faculty member who has watched you work every week for a year.

Presenting your results

Fellows present their cohort's results to college leadership and to the partner companies that fund the pods.

A voice in selection

Fellows help select the following year's cohort.

About pay and hours

Fellows are paid an hourly wage for coaching hours and for the training intensive. The commitment is about 10 hours a week during the semester, and we build the schedule around your courses. We pay hourly rather than by stipend because a stipend can count against need-based financial aid. If you receive aid, talk to us early anyway so we can check.Confirm

Boundaries

What the role does not include.

Coaching a design team means you will sometimes be the first person a student tells that something is wrong. Knowing where the role ends is part of the training, and it protects you as much as them.

You are not a counselor

If a student is in distress, your job is to stay with them and connect them to Counseling and Psychiatric Services or the Dean of Students office. You are not expected to handle it, and you should not try to.

You are not confidential

As a paid student employee you are a mandatory reporter. If a student begins to tell you about sexual misconduct or relationship violence, you have to say before they continue that you are required to report it. Training covers how to say this.Confirm

You are not a grader

You coach teams. You do not assign grades, adjudicate academic integrity, or decide who passes. Those belong to course faculty.

Someone is always reachable

There is a named person on call for anything outside what you were trained for. Using them is the expected behavior, not a failure.

You get this in writing on day one. What the role is and is not, a referral list with names and numbers, your legal obligations in plain language, your pay and hours, and the rubric we use when we sit in on your sessions. Nothing about how you are evaluated should be a surprise.

Oversight

How fellows are supported and reviewed.

Group supervision

Every other week, an hour with the other fellows. You bring a team that is stuck, or a conversation that went badly, and the group works on it. Most of what fellows learn happens here.

Two observations

Someone sits in on your session twice a semester and scores it against a rubric you already have. The main thing it looks at is whether you are asking questions or supplying answers.

If it is not going well

A private conversation about what was observed, then focused support, then a second look. Reassignment only if that does not work. Nothing about it is informal or unwritten.

Selection

How the process works.

Most fellows come from the undergraduate teaching assistants already working in EGR 100, and nominations come from course faculty. In later cycles current fellows will nominate as well. You can also apply on your own, and applying without a nomination does not put you at a disadvantage.

Step 1

Nomination or application

A short written application: your experience in EGR 100, why you want the role and one time you helped someone learn something hard.

Step 2

Teaching sample

Three minutes teaching us something you understand well, without slides. We are looking at how clearly you explain it.

Step 3

Coaching scenario

A short role-play with a first-year team whose design has a problem. Can you help without solving it for them?

Step 4

Panel

A conversation with course faculty, a current fellow and an engineer from a partner company.

Step 5

Selection Day

Each cohort is announced together, by name, before the semester begins.

If you are not selected: we tell you why. We invite strong applicants into tutoring, build-night and story-team roles, and give them priority in the next cycle.

Selection criteria

What we look for in an applicant.

You do not need a perfect transcript, and you do not need to have enjoyed every part of your first year. Having found it difficult is useful experience for this role.

What matters

  • You can explain something clearly to someone who does not have your background
  • You ask questions instead of supplying answers
  • You have worked on a team that went badly and can say what you learned
  • You notice when someone has stopped speaking up
  • You are interested in an industry and can say why, with specifics
  • You are reliable week to week

What matters less

  • A particular grade point average, as long as you are in good academic standing
  • Prior teaching or tutoring experience
  • Being the strongest technical student in your year
  • Knowing exactly what you want to do after graduation
  • Having a nomination in hand before you apply

Common questions

What applicants usually ask.

Can I do this alongside a project team or a job?
It is possible. 10 hours a week is a genuine commitment, and one role done well is better than three done badly. If your schedule is tight, tell us during selection and we will be honest about whether it works.
Do I have to be an expert in the design projects?
No. You are coaching how teams work and how they think, not supplying technical answers. Course faculty and graduate assistants cover the content.
Do I need to be a teaching assistant already?
It is the usual route in, since fellows are drawn from the teaching assistants supporting EGR 100. If you have not been one, say so in your application. Strong applicants who have not yet worked as a teaching assistant are pointed toward that first.
How is this different from being a teaching assistant?
You keep doing that work. The Fellowship adds partner-funded training before the semester, an additional stipend, an industry mentor, and assignment to specific design teams.
What year do I need to be in?
Fellows are upper-level students who have completed EGR 100. Second-year students are eligible, and students finishing their first year can be nominated.
What is an industry pod?
Fellows are grouped by field, such as aerospace or energy, with a partner company supporting that group. You are matched to a pod based on your interests, and your industry mentor comes from that company.
Does the partner company get access to my information?
Partner money pays for the coaching; it does not buy anything about you. If you want a company to know who you are, the Center for Spartan Engineering is the front door and it is worth using early.
Will this affect my financial aid?
It can, since paid campus employment counts toward your total aid package. Talk to us and to the Office of Financial Aid before you accept, and we will help you work it out.
What if I am not sure I am good enough?
Please apply anyway. Remembering what it felt like not to know is useful in this role.

Partners

Our Cornerstone Partners.

Michigan needs more engineers than it is currently producing, and the ones it needs will increasingly come from students nobody told this field was for them. GE Aerospace and Consumers Energy have been working on that with us for years. We call them Cornerstone Partners, after the design course at the centre of the first year.

Cornerstone Partner

Cornerstone Partner

What a partnership is for.

Growing the field

Michigan's graduating high school classes are getting smaller while the work waiting for engineers is getting broader. More of the engineers this state needs will have to come from students who were never told the field was for them. That is the shared problem, and it is bigger than any one company's hiring.

What only a company can give

Students believe practising engineers in a way they never believe a brochure. An engineer who says the first draft is always wrong, and means it, changes what a first-year thinks normal looks like. Nobody on our staff can be that person.

What only we can give

Every student who intends to be an engineer at MSU passes through these two courses. Roughly 1,900 people a year, reached before anyone has sorted themselves into who is doing well and who is quietly falling behind.

The long view

Our partners have been at this long enough that engineers they helped are now senior in their own fields. It is generational work, and the partners who treat it that way get the most out of it.

Week seven.

The two moments partners are already part of sit at either end of the semester. The challenge is set in September, the showcase is judged in December, and the gap between them is where teams get stuck.

What happens there

Teams have committed to an idea and are finding out what it will cost them. Scope is too big, something does not work, and nobody has told them this is normal. It is where an experienced engineer is worth the most and where there is currently nobody to be one.

What it looks like

One class period. Teams present where they are and what is in their way. Engineers sit at stations and work through it with them. No grade attached. Just two people looking at a problem.

The arithmetic

There are about 475 teams. An engineer works with four in an afternoon, so twenty engineers reach around eighty of them, roughly one team in six. Two partners plus alumni gets you to a third.

Why it costs almost nothing

The expensive part is your engineers' afternoon, not our budget. Materials run to a few dollars a team. This is the cheapest and most valuable thing in the partnership.

December, and what it takes to build properly.

Everyone builds to the same standard

Teams work to a modest materials allowance. When it runs out, what a team makes partly depends on whose parents can cover the difference. Twenty dollars a team fixes that across the whole course for under ten thousand dollars.

Judging, and the award only you can give

If your engineers saw teams in October and again in December, they are the only people who can judge how far a team travelled. Nothing else in the course makes that award possible.

Awards from inside the program

Some come from teammates, faculty and coaches instead of a judging panel. The person who made their team work. The student who kept going after a bad start. Grades see neither of them.

Families in the room

Most first-year engineering students have an engineer somewhere in the family. For the ones who do not, December is the first time anyone at home watches them do engineering. That is worth paying to make possible.

Money that reaches students directly.

Awards worth a course

Each award covers a three-credit class, so it lands against a bill a student is actually looking at. Most go to students at the edge of the calculus sequence, where the evidence says the money does the most work.

Coaches in the room

Paid undergraduates working inside the design sections during class, with every team, whether or not they asked. Sections run that way show substantially lower failure rates, and the gains are largest for women and first-generation students. Your engineers coach them twice a semester.

Tutors on the two hard subjects

Named tutor positions on mathematics and the computing course, five nights a week in Wonders. Maths placement predicts finishing an engineering degree better than anything else we could find.

Every student role is paid

An unpaid role quietly shuts out anyone who needs those hours for a job, and those are the students we most want doing it.

A named space that carries your work.

A room with your work in it

A lobby or build space carrying real problems and things students can pick up and look at. A name on a wall with nothing behind it becomes a plaque nobody reads.

Kept current

Two content changes a year, which also gives your people a reason to come back to the building.

The new building

The Leinweber Center for Engineering and Digital Innovation opens around the end of 2028. Cornerstone Partners are in that conversation first.

Spartan Stories.

The College keeps a running collection of what engineers who came through here actually did next. It is worth more to a first-year student than anything we could write about ourselves, for one specific reason.

The struggle is the useful part

In a controlled study, students who read about scientists' struggles improved their science grades over the following term. The group who read only about success did worse than before they started. A wall of triumph does real damage.

Who it reaches

Most first-year engineering students have an engineer somewhere in the family. The rest are working out whether this field is for people like them, and a stranger who did it first is the closest thing to the head start everyone else got at home.

What we ask for

The year that was hard. The course that nearly ended it. The thing you got wrong before you got it right. Those are the ones students actually read.

How the money actually works.

Priced by the unit

A coach, a tutor position, an award, a team's materials. Every line is a countable thing at a real price, so you can see exactly what your money buys.

Some of it deliberately unspent

Roughly one dollar in twelve is uncommitted at the start of the year and spent on what the year turns up. Most gifts are fully allocated a year before anyone knows what the year will require. We report where it went, including the times it did not work.

What we will tell you

What your money paid for, and what changed alongside it. We stop short of claiming we caused a retention number, because we cannot prove it and you would be right to doubt us if we tried.

Internships are the real bridge

First-year contact builds familiarity. The internship is where it becomes hiring. We report those two things separately.

EGR 100

Introduction to Engineering Design.

Two credits, one semester, and the only course shared across every engineering major at MSU. You are put on a team, given a problem with no answer key, and asked to defend what you built.

The semester

How the course runs.

Your team

You do not pick it. Teams are built deliberately, usually four or five students from different intended majors, using a tool that balances skills and working styles. The first session covers how a team works before it covers the project. Most teams hit a rough patch around week five, which is normal and part of what the course teaches.

The problem

Open-ended, with real constraints and no single correct solution. Partner companies supply some of the problems, so what you work on is a scaled-down version of something an engineer is being paid to solve.

Week 8

One class session set aside for a check-in. Everyone talks with someone about how the semester is going, whether or not anything is wrong, because the students who most need to raise a hand are the least likely to.

Design reviews

You present your thinking before it is finished and people ask why you made each choice. It is uncomfortable the first time. Explaining a decision under questioning is most of what practicing engineers do.

What you produce

A working prototype or model, written documentation, and a final presentation at the December showcase. The showcase is open, and working engineers judge it alongside faculty.

Who helps

Course faculty, graduate teaching assistants, and undergraduate teaching assistants who took the course a year or two ago and sit with teams in studio. Some of them are Design Coach Fellows, who work with specific teams across the semester.

About Design Coach Fellows →

Two credits is misleading. It is the smallest course on your schedule. Students still bring it up in interviews three years later, because it is where they first had something they built and could explain.

The design challenge

Where industry meets the first year.

Every first-year team spends the semester on one open problem carried in from working practice. It has no answer at the back, no worked example, and a real constraint attached. Teams present what they built in December to a room that includes people who solve problems like it for a living.

The problem comes from outside

A brief written by engineers who have lived with the problem. Students find out early that a real constraint is not a hurdle someone invented to make the assignment harder.

Partners stay with it

Cornerstone Partners set the brief in September, work with teams at the week-seven review when the scope has gone wrong, and judge the results in December alongside faculty.

The industry is wider than one company

Alumni across automotive, energy, aerospace, semiconductors and medical devices judge, mentor and answer the questions students send. The brief comes from one place; the field it opens onto does not.

What a brief looks likeSample

Shortening the time it takes to inspect a component between flights.

Inspection runs on a fixed schedule, whether or not anything needs attention, and equipment sitting still earns nothing. Teams were asked to shorten it without reducing what gets checked.

  • ConstraintsNothing that adds weight. Nothing that requires removing the component. Must work with the tools a technician already carries.

Things we got wrong, and changed. We have had this program looked at by people paid to find its faults, and they found several. We had built a plan with no mathematics in it, when maths placement predicts who finishes an engineering degree better than anything else we could find. We had been designing things for students to attend, until we counted the hours in a first-year week and found that students who work or commute or look after someone have almost none spare, which finally explained why the people who most need help are the least likely to turn up to it. And we had been implying that this program improves retention, which studied properly across a hundred institutions turns out to be a much weaker relationship than the field usually admits. So we stopped saying it. What we can say is narrower and steadier: these courses reach every student who arrives intending to be an engineer, before anyone has sorted themselves out, and nothing optional can do that.
A note on how the coaching role changed. We first designed this as coaches teams could call on when they needed one. Then we looked at what has actually been tested, and the version with real evidence behind it works differently: the coach is in the room during class, with every team in the section, whether or not they asked. Sections run that way show substantially lower failure rates, and the gains are largest for women and first-generation students. The version where teams have to ask reaches the teams confident enough to ask. So the coaches are part of the class now, not a service running alongside it, and they are paid, because unpaid roles quietly exclude anyone who needs the hours for work.

Program design

Why the first year is built this way.

CoRe is not a set of services that happen to run in the same year. A small number of moments carry most of the weight, each one has something attached to it inside EGR 100, and we measure whether it worked. This page is the reasoning.

The case

Why the first year matters.

Attrition in engineering concentrates between the first and second year, so most programs focus their attention there. Keeping students enrolled is necessary, and CoRe aims further than that, at the working habits and standards students carry into the rest of the degree and into the profession.

How you work

Whether you check your own reasoning, ask for help before something is urgent, and finish what you start. These become defaults quickly and are difficult to change later.

How you work with people

Your first team, your first design review, the first time you have to defend a decision out loud. Engineering is collaborative work, and year one is where students learn what that requires.

What good looks like

Students who see real engineering work early set their standards against it. Students who have only coursework to go on measure themselves against that instead, and adjust once they reach the profession.

Where the weight sits

The three moments.

Weeks 1 to 2
Arrival and first team

Students are assigned to a design team in the first days of EGR 100. Belonging gets set here, and so does team trouble. Teams are formed deliberately, not at random, and the first session covers how a team works before it covers the project.

Weeks 5 to 8
The first setback

First exams come back and most teams hit a rough patch. Everything at orientation has been forgotten by now. A required check-in inside EGR 100 puts tutoring, advising and support in front of students at the point they need them, not in week one.

Spring
Deciding whether to stay

Students choose a major and decide whether engineering is for them, usually on very little information about the work. Early contact with working engineers widens what they know exists and gives them a basis for the choice. What decides where they end up comes later, through internships and co-ops.

The mechanism

What is built into EGR 100.

Optional programming reaches the students who were already going to be fine. Each of the moments above has something attached to it inside EGR 100, so nobody has to know it exists in order to get it. Course faculty own it, and Design Coach Fellows run it in studio alongside the teams they already work with.

Week 1

A panel of upper-level students

A panel of second and third-year students talking about what surprised them, what they got wrong, and what they wish someone had told them. Aimed at everyone, because the unwritten rules are unwritten for the whole room.

Weeks 3, 7 and 11

Three short writing assignments

Students write about why the material connects to something they care about. It takes 15 minutes and it is graded for completion. The research behind this is some of the strongest in engineering education.

Week 8

A required check-in

One class session where every student talks with someone about how the semester is going. Students who are struggling are the least likely to raise a hand, so nobody has to.

Spring

A conversation with a working engineer

Every student talks with someone doing the work before they commit to a major. Course descriptions say what a class covers. They do not say what a Tuesday looks like, and students who have never met an engineer are choosing from a shorter list than they know.

Why it is built this way. Each of these has been tested in controlled studies elsewhere and moved grades and persistence, particularly for first-generation students. None requires a student to opt in, ask for help, or already know how the system works.Confirm

Access

Designed for students who do not live in the halls.

Engineering housing in South Neighborhood is a choice. A student who lives elsewhere, commutes, works long hours or cares for someone at home is the person a program like this usually serves worst. They are the person it is designed around.

Delivered during class time

The three moments happen during EGR 100, at a time every student is already on campus. Nothing important requires coming back in the evening.

No cost to take part

Tutoring, advising and the guide are free, and the support page names the offices that help when money is the problem.

Written down, not passed around

What students learn from each other in the halls is written down in the first-year guide, so a student who is not in the halls is not behind.

Student input

The student design team.

A small group of students works with CoRe staff each year on one part of the experience. They hold a budget and make a decision that is theirs, not a recommendation someone else can ignore. Groups that only advise tend to stop showing up.

Measurement

What we measure, and how.

Whether students come back for a second year is the floor, not the goal. What we want to know is whether they are becoming the kind of engineer the degree is for, which means measuring things retention numbers cannot see.

Belonging

A short set of questions in weeks 2 and 8 and at the end of the year, using items validated in engineering education research. Three points, so we can see a direction and not just one reading.Confirm

Engineering identity

Interest, recognition and confidence, measured with an established scale from engineering education research, not questions we wrote ourselves.Confirm

Team function

CATME, the peer evaluation tool built at Purdue for exactly this kind of course. It is already part of how teams are formed and reviewed, so the data exists without asking students for anything extra.Confirm

Help-seeking

Whether students use tutoring, advising and office hours, and how early in the semester they start. Existing records answer this without a survey.

Small on purpose. CoRe has no research staff, so we collect what a small team can read and act on, not a battery nobody has time to analyse. Nothing here has been measured yet. The first cohort begins fall 2026 and the first readout is December 2026.

It worked

A strip that changes color as a part wears down

Team 14 · Ava M., Daniel O., Priya K., Sam R. · GE Aerospace brief

The problem

Inspecting a component takes hours and happens on a schedule rather than when something needs attention.

What went wrong

Three weeks of materials that either did not change color or changed color immediately. The team nearly switched projects in week six.

What they did

Stopped looking for a material and started looking for a coating, which a graduate teaching assistant suggested after they described the dead end out loud.

Where it ended

First place. A GE engineer asked whether it would survive a wash cycle, and the team did not know. They said so.

PhotoSample
It partly worked

A checklist that routes a technician through an inspection

Team 3 · Marcus L., Jen T., Omar A. · GE Aerospace brief

The problem

The same brief, approached from the other end: not a faster inspection but a better-ordered one.

What went wrong

They built the app before asking anyone who does inspections what order they work in. The first version made the job slower.

What they did

Rebuilt it after a 20-minute call with a technician, which is the part they say they should have started with.

Where it ended

Second place. Judges noted it solved the scheduling problem and not the inspection itself, which the team had not realized.

PhotoSample
It did not work

A sleeve giving a camera a clear path into a tight space

Team 22 · Lena B., Chris D., Fatima H., Wei Z. · GE Aerospace brief

The problem

Reaching a section that normally requires taking part of the assembly apart.

What went wrong

The sleeve fit and the camera would not turn the corner. They found out two days before the showcase and could not fix it in time.

What they did

Presented it anyway, with a clear account of the geometry problem and what they would change.

Where it ended

Judges’ pick. One of them said afterward that explaining a failure well is most of the job.

PhotoSample
It worked

A rig that tests how long a seal lasts under vibration

Team 9 · Isabel R., Tomás V., Grace O., Ken A. · GE Aerospace brief

The problem

Nobody could say how long the seals last, because testing them takes longer than anyone has.

What went wrong

Two members did most of the work for the first month while the other two waited to be told what to do. It came out in the week 8 check-in.

What they did

Split the build into four pieces with names on them. The team says this was the actual turning point, not anything technical.

Where it ended

No placement, and the rig runs. Two of them are using it in a research lab this year.
